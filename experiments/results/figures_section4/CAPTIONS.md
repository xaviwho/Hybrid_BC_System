## Fig. 5 — Hybrid orchestration under concurrency

> **Fig. 5. Orchestration behaviour across four record fixtures.**
> **(a)** Throughput against client concurrency. All four fixtures transition at
> the same point, `c = 10`, which is the orderer's `BatchSize.MaxMessageCount`:
> below it every transaction waits out `BatchTimeout = 2 s` (time-cut regime),
> at and above it a block fills on count before the timer expires (count-cut).
> Because the harness holds exactly `c` requests in flight, the threshold is the
> message count itself, with no dependence on latency. `fabric_only` — the
> control fixture, which commits privately and enqueues no public anchor — is
> the only series that remains stable above the transition; the three anchoring
> fixtures oscillate and then collapse at `c = 32`.
> **(b)** Acknowledgement latency decomposed at the transition, `c = 8` against
> `c = 10`. The private-commit stage absorbs essentially the whole drop. The
> policy stage occupies a 3–38 ms band throughout, under 1.5% of acknowledgement
> latency, and is too thin to resolve at this scale. No anchor stage appears:
> anchoring is off the acknowledgement path entirely, delivered asynchronously by
> the relay after the 202 response.
> **(c)** Outbox insertion against concurrency. Insertion cost rises with load in
> every fixture, but `fabric_only` holds 53–92 ms where the anchoring fixtures
> reach 450–585 ms, isolating relay contention on the single SQLite writer as the
> mechanism. Insertion is never dominant: at `c = 32` for `all_public` it is
> 273.5 ms of a 5689.2 ms acknowledgement, 4.8%.
> Values above `c = 16` for the anchoring fixtures vary by 2–3× between runs at
> `TPUT_N = 50` and should be read as curve shape rather than point estimates.

## Fig. 7 — Merkle batching economics

> **Fig. 7. Anchoring cost against Merkle batch size, measured on-chain.**
> **(a)** Amortized gas per record, log–log, against the measured single-record
> anchor of 273,695 gas (dashed). The inset resolves the crossover the outer
> axis cannot: at `N = 1` batching costs **more**, 296,267 against 273,695 gas,
> because a one-leaf tree pays the root-anchor overhead without amortizing it.
> Root-anchor cost is flat in `N` (relative spread 3 × 10⁻⁵), which is why
> per-record cost falls as `1/N` — only the 32-byte root reaches the chain.
> **(b)** Reduction relative to the single-record anchor. The curve begins
> **negative** at −8.25% and crosses zero between `N = 1` and `N = 2`; batching
> is a loss below two leaves. It reaches 99.46% at `N = 200`.
> **(c)** Merkle proof size, which an auditor must carry: 0 B at `N = 1` rising
> to 256 B at `N = 200`, as 32⌈log₂ N⌉. Operator cost falls as `1/N` while
> verifier cost grows logarithmically, and that opposition is what bounds a
> useful batch size rather than any on-chain limit.

## Fig. 8 — Twin lifecycle

> **Fig. 8. Rollback cost and storage under the delta-plus-checkpoint model.**
> **(a)** Rollback latency against target version index, `q = 100`. Cost is
> **not** monotonic in `k`: it is sawtooth, tracking the number of delta
> applications `u` rather than the distance travelled. `u` is annotated at each
> point and is bounded by the checkpoint interval, `u ≤ q = 100`. The extremes
> are the trap — `k = 1` and `k = 1000` both land on `u = 0` and cost 0.104 ms
> and 0.120 ms respectively, so a first-versus-last probe measures nothing and
> would license a false constant-time claim. Marker shape and colour give the
> path the engine took: direct, checkpoint, or inversion from head.
> **(b)** Stored size against retained versions, both update conditions, with
> the snapshot-equivalent baseline dashed. Under sparse updates storage is
> 4.01× smaller at 800 versions, 95.9 B per version. Under dense updates the
> series lies **on** the baseline at 0.996× — parity, not a regression, because
> the `min(delta, snapshot)` fallback declines to store a delta larger than the
> snapshot it replaces. The saving is therefore conditional on update sparsity,
> and the fallback is what bounds the downside.
> **(c)** Exactly-once (unchanged): 71 requests — 6 sequential duplicates,
> 25 concurrent duplicates, 40 distinct — one anchoring transaction per
> idempotency key, no duplicates.

## Fig. 9 — Recovery path selection (new)

> **Fig. 9. Checkpoint restore against inversion from head, `q = 100`, sparse
> updates.** **(a)** Both paths across version history. Checkpoint restore is
> measured at all ten targets; inversion from head was measured only in the
> near-head region, and is plotted only where measured rather than interpolated.
> **(b)** The near-head region resolved. At every one of the four near-head
> targets inversion is roughly half the cost of checkpoint restore — at
> `k = 990`, 0.061 ms against 0.124 ms — because inversion applies `u = 11`
> deltas walking back from head where the checkpoint path applies `u = 89`
> walking forward. `u` is annotated on every bar and the star marks the path the
> engine selected, which is inversion at all four. Near-head targets are four of
> the ten measured, so the second path is not a corner case, and Eq. (34) is
> load-bearing rather than vestigial.

**Why Table VI's sign flipped.** The old values are exact `273,623/N` multiples,
so the old method took root-anchor gas to equal single-record gas and derived
reduction as `1 − 1/N` — which is why 90.00% / 95.00% / 99.50% are round. The
current run measures root gas independently: 296,267, flat in `N` to 3 × 10⁻⁵, and
**higher** than the 273,695 single-record anchor. Hence a real loss at `N = 1`. The
change is methodological, not noise.

**The storage claim needs writing, not correcting.** §IV-D currently disclaims a
saving. Fig. 8(b) now measures 4.01× at 800 versions, conditional on sparsity, with
dense updates at parity because `min(delta, snapshot)` declines a delta larger than
the snapshot it replaces. That is a Contribution 3 result the paper does not yet
claim.
